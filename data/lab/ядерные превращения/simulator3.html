Theory: Radioactive Decay

Radioactivity is the spontaneous transformation of one atomic nucleus into another, accompanied by the emission of various particles and radiation.

Key Concepts and Radiation Properties

Penetrating Power

Different types of radiation interact with matter differently, which determines their penetrating power. This is one of the most important characteristics of radiation, affecting protection methods.

[Image of penetrating power of alpha, beta, and gamma radiation]
  • Alpha particles (α): These are heavy, positively charged particles (helium nuclei). They rapidly lose energy upon collision with atoms and have the lowest penetrating power. Alpha radiation is completely stopped by a sheet of paper or even the outer layer of skin.
  • Beta particles (β⁻, β⁺): These are light charged particles (electrons or positrons). Their penetrating power is medium. They pass freely through paper but are effectively stopped by a thin layer of metal (e.g., a few millimeters of aluminum) or a thick layer of plastic.
  • Gamma rays (γ): These are high-energy photons (electromagnetic radiation) with no mass and no charge. They have the highest penetrating power and can pass through thick layers of dense materials. Significant layers of lead or concrete are required for protection against them.

Source of Electron in Electron Capture

In electron capture (often called K-capture), the nucleus does not emit a particle but instead captures one of its own atom's electrons. Most often, this is an electron from the K-shell—the energy level closest to the nucleus. Less frequently, electrons from the farther L or M shells are captured.

The K-shell is preferred because its electrons have the highest probability of "being" within the volume of the nucleus. After capture, a proton in the nucleus transforms into a neutron, and the vacant spot in the electron shell is immediately filled by electrons from higher levels. This transition is accompanied by the emission of characteristic X-rays.

Neutrino (ν) and Antineutrino (ν̄)

The neutrino and its antiparticle, the antineutrino, are fundamental particles with very small (but non-zero) mass and no electric charge. They only participate in the weak nuclear interaction (and gravity), which is why they interact extremely weakly with matter. They can fly through an entire planet almost without colliding with a single atom, earning them the nickname "ghost particles."

They are "born" in beta decay processes and are necessary to satisfy the laws of conservation of energy and momentum. Without them, calculations showed that some energy simply disappeared during decay. Neutrinos and antineutrinos "carry away" this missing energy:

  • In β⁻ decay (a neutron turns into a proton and an electron), an antineutrino (ν̄ₑ) is emitted.
  • In β⁺ decay (a proton turns into a neutron and a positron), a neutrino (νₑ) is emitted.
  • In electron capture (a proton captures an electron and becomes a neutron), a neutrino (νₑ) is also emitted.

1. Alpha Decay (α-decay)

Displacement Rule: The element transforms into a new element located 2 cells closer to the beginning of the periodic table.

23892U → 23490Th + 42He

2. Beta Decay (β⁻-decay)

Displacement Rule: The element transforms into a new element located 1 cell further towards the end of the periodic table.

146C → 147N + 0-1e + ν̄ₑ

3. Positron Decay (β⁺-decay)

Displacement Rule: The element transforms into a new element located 1 cell closer to the beginning of the periodic table.

2211Na → 2210Ne + 0+1e + νₑ

4. Electron Capture (K-capture)

Displacement Rule: The element transforms into a new element located 1 cell closer to the beginning of the periodic table.

4019K + 0-1e → 4018Ar + νₑ

5. Gamma Decay (γ-decay)

Displacement Rule: No element transformation occurs. The element remains in the same place.

6027Co* → 6027Co + γ
